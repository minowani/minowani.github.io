Nīvaraṇā

A popular translation for nīvaraṇā is hindrances.

Language is a bit ambiguous so understand that they may not feel as a hindrance to you but they are showstoppers to wisdom.

There are these five obstructions:

  1. Kāmacchanda
  2. Byāpāda
  3. Thinamiddha
  4. Uddhaccakukkucca
  5. Vicikicchā

1. Kāmacchanda

Kāmacchanda is a compound of kāma (lust) and chanda (interest) and the interest in lust is what normally drives one to find happiness when engaged with the world, not when drawn back from it. It depends on improper attention to an indication of attractiveness (subhanimittaṃ). The more that keeps being fed, the more it grows. The analogy given in SN 46.55 is about a bowl of water mixed with various dyes which when used as a mirror gives a distorted view. Thus when open to it we get enchanted by all the beautiful colourings. When fed it consumes, obsesses, giving less room for more beneficial things. Under its influence we turn away from doing what is wise to do.

2. Byāpāda

Byāpāda is an obstruction which depends on an indication of friction (paṭighanimittaṃ). With improper attention it appears and develops. The analogy given is about a bowl of water heated up which when used as a mirror gives a distorted view. The more heated up we are the less room we give for wise things. Heated up, out of friction, is generally an image for the display of anger. So let's call it just that.

3. Thinamiddha

Thinamiddha is a compound of thīna + middha. The dictionary lists for thīna stiffness, obduracy, stolidity, indifference. And for middha torpor, stupidity, sluggishness. This obstruction gets fed by improper attention to things such as dislike, discontentment, weariness or sloth (tandī), yawning (vijambhikā), drowsiness after a meal (bhattasammado), sluggishness (līnattaṃ) of mind (SN 46.51). It pops up when the strengths (eye, ear, etc.) are not held in check, when not wakeful, when not moderate in eating (AN 5.56). The analogy given is a bowl of water covered up with weeds which couldn't be used as a mirror. Weed takes time to grow so one had no interest and/or the energy in keeping it tidy. Of the listed words for thīna indifference would fit, as the other items seem to describe aspects. But indifferent is a bit ambiguous as it is also understood as equanimity, neutrality or objectivity, it is when you don't have a strong preference. So I prefer apathy. Apathy is when one isn't interested due to absence of emotion or enthusiasm. Drowsiness after a meal then belongs to middha, for which we normally use tiredness, fatigue, lethargy. Being sleepy or hungry can make one lethargic, which is characterized by fatigue, drowsiness and lack of energy and alertness and it is something to snap out of. So I think that with lethargy it is clearer what it is about. SN 46.52 shows that this obstruction can be split two obstruction, thīna and middha, thus for thinamiddha I use apathy plus lethargy.

4. Uddhaccakukkucca

Uddhaccakukkucca is a compound of uddhacca and kukkucca. Uddhacca is listed as agitation, excitement, distraction, flurry. And kukkucca with bad doing, bad character, remorse, scruple, flurry, worry, brooding. The analogy is a bowl of water being stirred which when used as a mirror would give a distorted view. And stirring up is what to excite or agitate is. This obstruction is fed by improper attention to things that bring no ease of mind (cetaso avūpasamo). SN 46.52 shows it can be split into these two as separate obstructions. Uddhacca, says AN 10.103, is what you get when paying to much improper attention to an indication of friction. It is from friction but not as with irritation, anger, which belong to the second obstruction, and not as dullness or sluggishness as that belongs to the third obstruction. Further is uddhacca one of the lasts yokes to go, thus present for the anāgāmin. So, with the stirring movement there is distorted view, if it is kept as it is. And this inactivity, or little activity, or the tendency not to change what is happening, is what inertia is. And inertia is based on friction. Kukuucca has quite a range in meaning while it is from improper attention to things that don't bring an ease of mind. And it also seem to be linked with fruit-buds (AN 4.196). Brooding is the one word in the list with a link to offspring and with feeling uncomfortable, worried, as if something bad is going to happen. Hence for uddhaccakukkucca I use inertia plus brooding.

5. Vicikicchā

Vicikicchā comes from vi+cikicchati, dis-reflect. The analogy says one can't use a bowl with muddy water in the dark as mirror. Thus a visual distinction can't be made. All look the same; without clarity, obscure, vague. When this as yoke is dealt with then there is that clarity and then there is no doubt (kankhā). With doubt things might not be so clear, but at least they are clear enough to have doubt about them; they can be seen or considered (there is just no certainty about them) but when vague, lacking distinction, then as such it isn't even considered, thus there is then not even doubt; unseen it will not be reflected on. Unclarity thus. This obstruction is fed by the improper attention to clarity lacking principles.

In Common

These five obstructions are all fed (to appear or grow) by improper attention:

  • interest in lust, by improper attention to an indication of attractiveness.
  • anger, by improper attention to an indication of friction.
  • apathy plus lethargy, by improper attention to dislike, weariness, not moderate in eating, etc.
  • inertia plus brooding, by improper attention to what brings no ease of mind.
  • unclarity, by improper attention to clarity lacking principles.

And thus are with paying proper attention, e.g. when honestly studying the texts, the obstructions absent (SN 46.38).

Gradual

We can play with this a little and say there is a certain sequence to it. We quickly learn that we should not prioritize lust. Then by not paying proper attention, as it is not quite clear and an alternative is not so easily met, we are so to speak left with irritation, frustration, anger (same metric-scale). Then by not paying proper attention, not dealing it rightly, yet not giving in to that either as we know getting angry is no good either, we are left with apathy and lethargy, a lack of interest, energy and enthusiasm. And by not dealing with it rightly we are left with inactivity, inertia and feeling uncomfortable, worried, sad. And hanging in that state of mind things don't become clearer. But by paying proper attention to things in our own domain, we cut a lot out which would have us focused and worried of the outside which then already gives us some ease. And this already gives some clarity when looking into them, which gives some energy, which gives pleasantness, with which comes relaxation, which allows for more concentration, which helps more to see how the things are, more clarity thus. And so this sequence also reinforces itself. So, as the obstructions spiraling build suffering up, the factors of enlightenment spiraling build suffering down. In this way we can see the second and third noble truth at play. This too is just about the rise and cessation of suffering.

Pāḷi-English

Nīvaraṇā obstructions.
Kāmacchanda interest in lust.
Byāpāda anger.
Thinamiddha apathy plus lethargy.
Uddhaccakukkucca inertia plus brooding.
Vicikicchā unclarity.
