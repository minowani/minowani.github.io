Brahmavihārā

Mettā, karuṇā, muditā and upekkhā are called the four brahmavihārā, often translated as divine abodes or abidings, sublime attitudes, abodes of Brahma.

Brahma is according to the texts the name of a higher god and vihāra means dwelling, as we are already used seeing viharati in 'On one occasion the exalted one dwells', so brahmavihāra could mean 'Dwellings of Brahma'. And this to indicate that if one acts like Brahma, have a mindset like Brahma, then one might end up in such situation. This goes for anyone (MN 96). These principles are seen as godly, divine, saintly, holy and are thus higher than what people usually would cultivate or aim for (see also "Ariyasāvaka").

These four dwellings are:

Mettā

Mettā is often found translated as loving-kindness, benevolence, goodwill. From "From Another Tradition (AN 3.69)" we know mettā goes against hate. It is said that mettā has to do with love and love and hate are a famous pair. Love doesn't quite fit as it is a bit ambiguous. Perhaps then kindness and the lot got being used. The Pāḷi might have different words, separate for what we see here as ambiguous and thus it may not be ambiguous there. Which might give us some playing room. So, back to love. Love differs from loving, which differs from to be in love, which differs from lover, and so there is this range with love which may or may not quite fit. But if I say 'lovingness' it is already understood differently as it is a quality of mind which is more about showing, indicating, care out of love. A mind filled with lovingness? Well, why not? Lovingness seems to fit just fine.

Karuṇā

Karuṇā is often translated as compassion. It is said to mean pity, compassion and sometimes we can find sympathy. It gives up vihesā (MN 62) which might mean something like fright. The dictionary gives a whole range for vihesā, including harm, cruelty, but it might be the case that vihesā can come from those as vihesikā, the adjective, can mean frightened. But for now we won't be looking into this as the difference is not needed here. When the texts talk about compassion the Pāḷi word is anukampa and not karuṇā. And another problem, at least that I have with it, is that pity, compassion, sympathy depend on someone else's misfortune. So they may be good qualities but is it what is meant here? The Brahma quality we could take as having a mind like Brahma, is higher than that of many other gods. So a good quality in itself doesn't mean it is high enough to be considered Brahma's. And such a divine mind, what could it entail? We already have lovingness and we have to come up with three more. But as there is not much to go by let's continue with the other two first to see if this helps sorting things out.

Muditā

Muditā is often translated as empathetic or appreciative joy. But for a higher state of mind this looks a bit to disturbing to me. It might be godly like but is it enough to be Brahma like? The dictionary says it has to do with soft-heartedness, kindliness, sympathy. However, in AN 3.96 we find that muditā is when there is no quarrelling, when the company is in unity, that it is as milk blending with water. It goes against arati (AN 6.113) of which the dictionary says it is something like dislike, discontent, aversion. Perhaps we can use 'harmony' here. To dwell in harmony, wouldn't that be divine?

Upekkhā

Upekkhā is often translated as equanimity. Equanimity can be used to indicate an evenness of mind, balance, calmness. MN 62 shows it gives up friction. It is also used for in the third and fourth radiance. Upekkhā is said to come from upa (near, upon, by, above) and ikkha (to see, to look) and meaning "looking from above". Which then could be more about the viewpoint, to how things are seen. Then, for looking at things from a distance, objectively would here fit. That by withdrawing things can be looked at more objectively, and thus more clearly, is not something we can't understand. So we might say that first we get to look at the world objectively and then from the radiances on we are getting to look objectively at aspects we consider to be of ourselves. And to not to take everything to heart, not take everything personal, but to act and look at things from objectiveness, doesn't that already look more saintly like?

Back to Karuṇā

So far then we got lovingness, karuṇā, harmony, objectiveness, of which the instruction talked gradually before getting to right meditation. So, from lovingness withdrawn we would get karuṇā, from karuṇā withdrawn harmony, from harmony withdrawn objectiveness. Cultivating one it pours over into the other. And in this way it is a refining, a gradual stilling, a going higher. As there seems to be not much to go, by we have to make an estimate guess for karuṇā. If possible I would like to stay in the range of compassion, pity, without the misfortune part; the whole unwholesomeness is here already left behind. As a greater distance to lovingness, since we are talking about principles, it must then be possible to see traces of this in every days' usage too. And we can see that people who say they are in love (which involves lovingness) can fall out of it. And if we stay at the good side of things it can be that a certain amiability or friendship is what can remain indicating this greater distance. Karuṇā, says the dictionary, compare it with the Vedic karuṇa nt. (holy) action. So, acting out of kindness then perhaps? A mind filled with kindness, would that be anything deity like?

Brahmavihārā

So we then got lovingness, kindness, harmony, objectiveness. This does flow, right? In the Aṅguttara Nikāya and Saṃyutta Nikāya these four are seldom called brahmavihārā and SN 54.11 shows that the concentration from meditation on the in and out breathe is also a brahmavihāra. Thus the brahmavihāra are not the four, but the four are brahmavihārā; it is a dwelling from a higher principle what makes a dwelling Brahma's dwelling (see also "Bhava"). When the world is seen in from this objectiveness the avarice and distress about it falls away, not from a disorderly kind of disinterest or dissociation but through this care, through lovingness. If we take ourselves out of the world, through objectiveness, where then to dwell in? Well, in body, when looking into body; in feelings ... mind .... principles, and that is how the instruction did continue. Thus establish a dwelling from right exertion (in 'goodness') then establish a dwelling from right meditation (in body, feelings, mind, principles) and then establish a dwelling from right concentration (in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th radiance). See also "Dripping Soap".

Pāḷi-English

Brahmavihārā dwellings of Brahma.
Mettā (f.) lovingness.
Karuṇā (f.) kindness.
Muditā (f.) harmony.
Upekkhā (f.) objectiveness.
